Attestation and apostille: why your company documents need them

25 Nov 2026 · 3 min read

A document signed in one country is not automatically accepted in another. Apostille and attestation are the two ways to prove it is genuine. Here is what each one means, when you need it and how to avoid delays.

You have formed your company, or you are about to take over one, and now a notary, an investment authority or a bank in another country asks for your documents "apostilled" or "attested". Many founders hear these words for the first time at this point, often when a deadline is close.

This guide explains what the two terms mean, when they come up and how to prepare.

Why a foreign document needs extra proof

An official in one country can't easily check a stamp or signature from another country. Before they rely on a certificate of incorporation, a power of attorney or a passport copy issued abroad, they want proof that the signature and seal on it are genuine.

Apostille and attestation give that proof. They confirm who signed or sealed the document. They do not confirm that what the document says is correct, and they don't replace the checks the receiving authority makes.

What an apostille is

An apostille is a single certificate added to a document by a designated authority in the country where the document was issued. It is used between countries that are part of the Hague Apostille Convention.

When both the issuing country and the receiving country are members, the apostille is usually all that is needed. There is no extra embassy step.

What attestation (legalisation) is

When the receiving country does not accept apostilles, or asks for its own embassy stamp, documents go through attestation, also called legalisation. This is a chain of stamps, typically:

  • A notary or the issuing office certifies the document or copy.
  • A government department in the issuing country confirms that signature.
  • The embassy or consulate of the receiving country adds its own attestation.

Each step depends on the one before it, so one wrong stamp or a mismatched name can send the document back to the start. Some countries also ask for a certified translation.

When our clients need them

Not every formation needs either. For a US LLC or a UK company, your passport and your details are usually all you send. Where we do see them on our website:

  • Netherlands BV: the company is set up through a Dutch notary, so passport copies must be certified with an apostille. We arrange this at cost.
  • Saudi Arabia: the Saudi company is owned by a foreign company, whose documents are attested by the Saudi embassy, together with a legal power of attorney. Attestation fees are paid separately at cost.
  • Established companies: when you take over one of our established companies, all documents are fully attested and the originals are couriered to your country.
  • Pakistan: to take over an established company, non-Pakistanis provide a passport and an attested power of attorney.

Tips to avoid delays

  • Ask the receiving authority, bank or notary exactly what they need before you start: apostille, embassy attestation, translation, or nothing.
  • Use the same spelling of names and addresses on every document.
  • Plan for the extra time. Attestation runs through several offices, and some steps need original documents sent by courier.
  • Keep the attested originals safe. Many authorities want to see the originals, not a copy.
