Shopify store setup: what your company and store need before launch

16 Nov 2026 · 3 min read

A Shopify store opens in an afternoon. A store that can take payments, ship orders and pass provider checks needs some groundwork. Here is what to sort out first, for your company and for the store itself.

Opening a Shopify store is quick. Running one that can accept card payments, pay out to a business account and ship to customers abroad takes some preparation. Many problems at launch come from details that don't match: a store in one name, a company in another, a payout account in a third.

Here is what to have in place before you go live.

1. Decide who is selling

Start with one question: are you selling your own brand, or reselling products made by other brands? A private-label brand usually wants a company, and later a trademark, in the market it sells to. A reseller needs to show where the products come from, such as invoices from suppliers or authorised distributors. The answer shapes the rest of your setup.

2. Choose the company behind the store

Your store's legal name, address and payouts should all match one company. Which company makes sense depends on where your customers are and where you live:

  • Selling mainly to US customers: many founders use a US LLC with an EIN.
  • Selling to the UK and Europe: a UK limited company is a common choice.
  • Selling mainly in Malaysia: a Malaysian Sdn. Bhd. may fit better.

Forming a company does not give you a visa or residency in that country.

3. Plan your payments early

Which payment options your store can use depends on the country where your business is registered and on each provider's own checks. Shopify Payments, Stripe, PayPal and others review your business before they accept you, and may ask for more documents later. Keep your company documents, your ID and a business account in the company's name ready. No one can guarantee an approval: the decision belongs to the provider.

4. Get the store details right

  • Business details: the company's legal name, address and contact details, exactly as on your company documents.
  • Policies: refund, shipping, privacy and terms of service pages that describe how you really work.
  • Product pages: clear descriptions, real photos, prices in your customer's currency and realistic delivery times.
  • Domain and email: your own domain and a business email address.

Payment providers look at your store as well as your documents. A store with missing policies or unclear products is harder to approve.

5. Set up shipping that matches your stock

Decide where your stock sits and who ships it: you, your supplier or a fulfilment partner. Then set your shipping zones and rates to match. If you sell abroad, check the import rules and duties for your products in each market.

6. Know your tax questions

Sales tax, VAT and income tax depend on where your company is, where your customers are and how much you sell. Some markets require tax registration once you pass a threshold. Speak to a qualified tax professional about your own situation.
